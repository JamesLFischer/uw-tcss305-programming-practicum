Assignment Overview:
This assignment focuses on designing and implementing a simulation of vehicle behaviors on a dynamic city grid using object-oriented programming principles such as inheritance, polymorphism, and encapsulation. The primary goal is to create a hierarchy of vehicle classes (Truck, Car, Taxi, ATV, Bicycle, and Human) with distinct behaviors for movement, collision handling, and interactions with the environment. Each vehicle must operate within specific constraints, such as terrain types and traffic light conditions, while adhering to its unique rules for choosing directions and responding to collisions.

The scope extends beyond basic implementation by requiring an abstract parent class, AbstractVehicle, to encapsulate shared behaviors and reduce redundancy. Additionally, the assignment emphasizes modularity and maintainability through the use of interfaces, abstract methods, and unit testing. Writing unit tests, particularly for the Truck class, ensures that vehicle behaviors are predictable and align with the given specifications.

The assignment also introduces GUI integration, where vehicles interact visually on a simulated city map, providing an opportunity to see the practical application of the implemented logic. This simulation reinforces the importance of correctness, as any errors in logic or movement can disrupt the animation or violate the defined behaviors.

Overall, the assignment encourages critical thinking about software design, testing, and debugging, while offering hands-on experience in applying advanced programming concepts in Java to build a realistic and functional simulation.

Technical Impression:
To complete this assignment, I first reviewed the project requirements to fully understand the behaviors and interactions expected from each vehicle type. I began by implementing the AbstractVehicle class, which serves as the foundation for all vehicle subclasses. This abstract class encapsulated common functionality such as movement, collision handling, and revival logic, reducing redundancy and ensuring consistency across the hierarchy.

I then developed individual subclasses for Truck, Car, Taxi, ATV, Bicycle, and Human, carefully tailoring their behaviors to the specifications. This included their unique rules for movement, terrain traversal, and responses to traffic lights and collisions. One of the challenges was implementing the chooseDirection method, as each vehicle required distinct logic to prioritize movement directions. Balancing the need for clear logic while adhering to the recommended cyclomatic complexity limits required significant effort, but helper methods simplified this process.
